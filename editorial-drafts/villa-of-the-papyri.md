# Villa of the Papyri

The Villa of the Papyri was a Roman seaside residence near [Herculaneum](https://dechive.wiki/knowledge/70743ac2-40c5-4082-b7ed-cfdeea7cce93). Buried during the AD 79 eruption of [Mount Vesuvius](https://dechive.wiki/knowledge/d51869b1-3dba-437c-b7c8-10441ebb710a), it became known for its remarkable collection of sculptures and the carbonised scrolls that give it its name. The [Herculaneum Papyri](https://dechive.wiki/knowledge/d41f8bb1-91d5-449b-ac64-842c2af98e52) provide evidence of ancient reading and philosophy; the villa itself reveals the setting in which a library and works of art formed part of elite Roman life.

## A residence buried by an eruption

The villa stood outside Herculaneum, facing the Bay of Naples. Like the neighbouring town and [Pompeii](https://dechive.wiki/knowledge/31ecf978-4412-4de1-a056-2b83c2533372), it was affected by the eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79. Volcanic deposits buried the building, and the surviving papyri were carbonised. Their preservation was a consequence of a disaster that destroyed communities around the volcano.

The identity of the villa's owner remains uncertain. It has been associated with Lucius Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus, Julius Caesar's father-in-law, but that attribution is a proposal rather than an established fact.

## Rediscovery underground

Explorers rediscovered the villa in the eighteenth century and investigated it through subterranean tunnels during the 1750s and 1760s. This method gave access to rooms and objects while much of the building remained buried. Karl Weber documented the site in a plan that later became important for understanding its layout.

Excavation resumed in the 1990s and early 2000s, revealing additional parts of the building. The villa has not been completely uncovered, so any account of its full extent and contents depends on evidence gathered in different periods and under different conditions.

## Scrolls, sculpture and Roman life

The archaeological park describes more than 1,800 carbonised papyri associated with the villa. Counts vary between sources because the surviving collection includes rolls and fragments; the figure should not be read as a count of complete, readable books. Many texts opened so far are works of Greek philosophy, including writings by Philodemus, a follower of Epicurus.

The residence also contained a substantial collection of bronze and marble sculpture. Getty's exhibition describes about ninety works, including images of mythological figures, athletes, rulers, poets and philosophers. Gardens, walkways and decorated spaces formed a setting for leisure, conversation and the display of learning.

## A surviving source of knowledge

The villa connects the material remains of a Roman residence to texts that might otherwise have disappeared. The [Herculaneum Papyri](https://dechive.wiki/knowledge/d41f8bb1-91d5-449b-ac64-842c2af98e52) are extremely fragile: historical attempts to open them sometimes damaged them, while modern imaging allows researchers to examine some writing without physically unrolling a scroll. Reading and interpreting the results remains specialist work.

The building's influence extends beyond archaeology. The Getty Villa in California was modelled on the Villa of the Papyri, using Weber's eighteenth-century plan alongside features drawn from other ancient sites. It is a modern interpretation, not the excavated Roman villa itself.
