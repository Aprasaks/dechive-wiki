# Herculaneum Papyri

The Herculaneum papyri are a collection of ancient scrolls and fragments recovered from the Villa of the Papyri near [Herculaneum](https://dechive.wiki/knowledge/70743ac2-40c5-4082-b7ed-cfdeea7cce93). Carbonised during the eruption of [Mount Vesuvius](https://dechive.wiki/knowledge/d51869b1-3dba-437c-b7c8-10441ebb710a) in AD 79, they preserve works of ancient philosophy and other writing. Their survival presents a difficult problem: many scrolls are too fragile to open without risking serious damage.

## A library buried by Vesuvius

The Villa of the Papyri was a large Roman residence outside Herculaneum. Eighteenth-century explorers reached it through underground passages and discovered a collection of charred papyrus rolls. The archaeological park describes more than 1,800 carbonised papyri associated with the villa. That number refers to the recovered collection, which includes fragments as well as rolls; it does not mean that every item is a complete, readable book.

The villa remains only partly excavated. Its library is an unusual surviving collection from classical antiquity, but the surviving material should not be treated as a complete record of every work once held there.

## Why the scrolls are difficult to read

Papyrus is a writing material made from strips of the papyrus plant. A scroll contains long sheets rolled into layers. At Herculaneum, heat transformed the papyrus into brittle, carbonised material. The layers can be compressed, distorted or stuck together, and physical unrolling can break them.

Earlier attempts to open the rolls produced readable texts but also damaged material. Many remain closed or only partly opened. Even when a surface becomes visible, missing pieces, faint writing and uncertainty in the surviving letters can make interpretation difficult.

## Reading without opening

Researchers have developed methods known as virtual unwrapping. First, X-ray computed tomography records a three-dimensional image of the object. Researchers then trace individual layers in the scan and use software to flatten those surfaces digitally. The resulting image can be examined for traces of writing without physically unrolling the scroll.

The task is not simply a matter of photographing a hidden page. Carbon-based ink may be difficult to distinguish from the carbonised writing surface. Researchers combine imaging, computational analysis and specialist interpretation to identify and assess possible text. Different scrolls and inks present different challenges.

## Recent research

In 2024, the Vesuvius Challenge reported the recovery of substantial passages from a scanned Herculaneum scroll. The work followed years of research into imaging, virtual unwrapping and identifying ink in scans.

In 2025, the Bodleian Libraries reported an image showing part of the interior of its scroll PHerc. 172, including columns of text. The scroll had been scanned in 2024. Researchers used computational techniques to improve the image, while scholars worked to transcribe and interpret the writing. The library noted that the ink in this particular scroll appeared more clearly in X-ray scans than ink in some other examples.

A 2026 research preprint reported complete virtual unwrapping and reading of another scroll, PHerc. 1667, using stated coverage and scholarly review criteria. As a preprint, that report should be distinguished from findings established through later peer review. The progress does not mean that every Herculaneum scroll has been read, or that a digital image removes the need for conservation and scholarly interpretation.

## Why the papyri matter

The surviving texts offer evidence about ancient intellectual life, including Greek philosophy in the Roman world. Individual works and fragments can add to, revise or complicate knowledge drawn from later manuscript traditions. Their interpretation depends on the quality of the evidence and on the work of papyrologists, historians, conservators and imaging researchers.

The collection also connects the history of a volcanic disaster to the history of books: the same eruption that destroyed [Pompeii](https://dechive.wiki/knowledge/31ecf978-4412-4de1-a056-2b83c2533372) and buried Herculaneum left fragile material that is still being studied today.
